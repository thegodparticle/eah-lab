Let's look at the different kinds of work that go into building a full Canvas course. Stage one is design. This is the pedagogical work that happens before any clicking, mapping alignment, sequencing modules, applying universal design. Stage two is building content. Recording the lectures, building the slides, curating the readings, captioning the media. For a fifteen-week graduate course, this turns into dozens of pages, dozens of files, and hours of captioning that has to be accurate. Stage three is building assessments. Writing the items, building the rubrics, setting up question banks. Stage four is publishing to Canvas. Building the modules, creating the pages, handling accessibility. Finally, stage five is running the term. Once the course is live, announcements go out every week, submissions need grading, discussions need facilitating. This is the part of the work that does not end until the semester does. However, students see only stage five. Administrators see only stage five. Faculty do all of this, every semester, for every course they teach. Let me show you what this work actually costs.

These are hidden behind every one of the stages I just walked through. One. Time. The median is seventy hours just to build a first online course. Then about twelve and a half hours per week to run it. And here is the part that gets you. Roughly half of that weekly work scales linearly with class size, about thirty minutes per student, per week. A section of forty students is twenty hours a week. Multiply by your teaching load. Two. The expertise gap. The research shows that only twenty-eight percent of faculty feel fully prepared for online course design. That number has been flat since the pandemic. Three. Design. Canvas's editor is not a free environment. By design, it strips JavaScript, custom CSS, and animation when you save. So if you want anything interactive on a page, a slider, a tabbed reading, an embedded simulation, you have two real options. Learn HTML and CSS, which freeCodeCamp puts at three hundred hours just to reach the beginner floor. Or pay a vendor. Over seven hundred institutions pay Cidi Labs to bridge that gap. Four. Accessibility. April twenty-six, twenty twenty-seven. The Department of Justice's Title Two final rule, which requires every Canvas page, every PDF, every video at our institution to meet WCAG two point one Level AA. One year from now. And to put the work into context, the WebAIM Million project measured the open web this year. Almost ninety-six percent of pages fail WCAG today. Five. Maintenance. Most of us do not teach a new course every year. We teach the same course again and again. And every time we do, the world around it has shifted. Pew Research measured this in twenty twenty-four. Thirty-eight percent of webpages from ten years ago no longer load. Now look at your Canvas course. The links you embedded five years ago. The video you assigned three years ago. The reading you scanned in twenty nineteen. Some fraction of those are already dead. And it is not just links. The AI policy in the syllabus did not exist in twenty twenty-two. The course is the same. The world around it is not. These do not sit separately. They compound. Time, the expertise gap, the design constraints, the accessibility deadline, the maintenance tax. Every course, every August. That is what every faculty member in this room is dealing with. So the question is the obvious one.

Component three. Your customization layer. Where you write your own rules, checks, and decision points. Three mechanisms, one at a time. First, skills. Think of a skill the way you think of a checklist you hand to a teaching assistant. Step by step, here is how a task gets done in our course. A skill is the same thing, written for Claude instead of for a TA. It is a markdown file. On the right, what one looks like. YAML at the top tells Claude when to use it. Below that, ordered phases in plain prose. On the left, what happens when I ask Claude to build a Canvas page. The skill loads, the references load, phase one begins. Other skills you might write: build a quiz from objectives, draft a weekly announcement in your voice, generate a rubric from a template you already use. Second, hooks. Think of a hook like a spell-checker that runs every time you save. Define the rule once, it runs forever. A hook is a small script that fires every time Claude writes or edits a file. On the right is the configuration in settings dot J S O N. The matcher says which tools to fire on. On the left, what happens when Claude writes a page that fails your accessibility check. The hook blocks the write. Claude cannot move on until the file passes. Enforcement that runs without you lifting a finger. Third, gates. Think of a gate like a stop sign at every intersection. The AI has to pause and wait for you to wave it through. GATE is a convention you write into every phase of every skill. On the right, the SKILL dot M D excerpt: each phase ends with a GATE directive that tells Claude to present the work and wait. On the left, what you see in your terminal. Four letters. C continue, R revise, S skip, N next phase only. You type one. The decision stays yours, every phase. Compare back to the chat-and-copy pattern. With web AI, your standards live in your head. Format breaks on every paste. You are in the loop only at the prompt. You re-prompt every session. With skills, hooks, and gates, your standards live in markdown on disk. Hooks validate every write. You stay in the loop at every phase. One skill works on every course you teach. You write the standards once. They run on every page, every course, every term. Two zones. Two answers.

When AI helps with course pages vs when AI helps with student work, the protection looks different.
Zone 1 · Authoring
No student data enters.
Pages, modules, syllabi, announcements. The AI works on artifacts, not on people.

The honest answer depends on what you ask the AI to do. There are two zones. Zone one, on the left. Authoring. Pages, modules, syllabus, announcements. When I ask the AI to build a Canvas page from outcomes, roll due dates, or write the Friday lab announcement, the AI never sees a student name. Nothing to leak. FERPA is not in scope for this kind of work. Zone two, on the right. Student data. Grading, summarizing submissions, analytics. This is the harder case, because PII does enter the loop. This is where canvas-mcp does the work. Vishal Sachdev built source-level anonymization into canvas-mcp. The function is anonymize underscore response underscore data. It runs on every API call to a student-data endpoint. Before the response reaches the model, names get hashed. Student underscore eight hex characters. Emails, login IDs, SIS IDs, integration IDs, avatar URLs, bios, time zones, locales, all redacted. The Canvas numeric ID is preserved because canvas-mcp needs it for follow-up operations, but it is not a name a person can read. On the left, what Canvas sends. Maria Rivera, mrivera at usouthal, login mrivera forty-two, SIS J zero zero one two three four five six. On the right, what Claude receives. Student underscore five nine nine four four seven one a. Same record. The work is preserved. The identifier is gone. So I can ask Claude to grade twenty-two submissions and it will evaluate the work without knowing whose work it is. The hash is deterministic SHA two-five-six. The real Canvas numeric ID is preserved alongside the hashed name. When I am done, I take the Canvas roster, hash each student ID myself, and match Student underscore five nine nine four four seven one a back to Maria for grade entry. Re-identification happens on my machine. The model never sees the link. The flag is ENABLE underscore DATA underscore ANONYMIZATION. The source defaults to true. Faculty stays the school official. The AI is the tool. canvas-mcp keeps the boundary.
Accessibility · the 2027 recipe
DOJ Title II · WCAG 2.1 AA · Apr 26, 2027 · WebAIM Million 2026

One skill file. Four rules. Any course.

FERPA was the first compliance question. Accessibility is the second. Write the rules once. The AI applies them across every page.
accessibility-skill.md .claude/skills/ ~80 lines
# accessibility-skill # Apply whenever the user asks about Canvas pages. # Each section pauses at the GATE for a yes. ## 1. FERPA was the first compliance question. This is the second. April twenty-six, twenty twenty-seven. The Department of Justice Title Two deadline for public university web content. Mobile is well over the fifty thousand population threshold the rule uses, so the University of South Alabama is in scope. Every Canvas page in every course we teach has to meet WCAG two point one Level AA by that date. The answer is on the left. One markdown file. About eighty lines. Lives in your project's dot claude slash skills folder. The AI loads it whenever you touch a Canvas page and applies every rule in it. Section one. Color contrast. WCAG one point four point three. Eighty-four percent of pages on the public web fail this. For every text node, compute contrast against the background. If less than four-point-five to one, propose a darker color. GATE per fix. Section two. Alt text. WCAG one point one point one. Fifty-three percent fail. Walk every image and PDF, draft alt from the caption and surrounding context, flag any scanned PDF that needs OCR. GATE per image, because alt text is judgment-heavy. The AI gets it close. You decide whether close is right. Section three. Headings and link text. WCAG one point three point one for headings, two point four point four for links. Forty-six percent of the web fails empty-link text, the click-here problem. Keep heading order sequential, no jumps from h2 to h4. Rewrite every vague link to describe where it goes. GATE per diff. Section four. The legal trap, worth pausing on. The Title Two rule has a third-party content exception. Faculty read it and assume it covers anything they did not author. It does not. If the syllabus requires a publisher portal, an embedded simulation, a third-party video, those are inside the scope. You picked them. The rule lists every external tool the syllabus requires, finds its accessibility statement, and FLAGS any that fail. The GATE here is whether to keep using the tool at all. On the right of the slide you see what each rule produces. Gray body text turning dark. An empty alt becoming a real description. A skipped heading becoming sequential. An unaudited publisher link becoming one with a documented status. Same shape as everywhere else in this talk. The AI proposes. The GATE pauses. You decide. The compliance work that used to be a once-a-year audit project becomes an ongoing pass that runs whenever you touch a page.

Caution · the same API works both ways
sources verified: Instructure docs · Oct 2025 product blog

Everything I just showed you, any student in your course can do too.

The Canvas REST API does not distinguish a faculty token from a student token.
The reality
The API is symmetric.
A student can mint a personal access token in under a minute. The same endpoints I demoed expose their own quiz attempts. List the quiz. Read the questions and the answer choices. Submit. Instructure publicly confirmed in October 2025:
“students setting up AI integrations to automatically review and complete assignments on their behalf.”
community.canvaslms.com / strengthening-security blog (Oct 2025)
The answer
Design beats detection.
Proctoring tools cannot see an API client running off-machine. The durable fix is two-layered. At the institution, admins can disable student access tokens with one permission flip. At the course, the answer is assessment design: in-person proctored fact recall, frequent low-stakes formative work, project-based application, oral defense.
Stanford Teaching Commons · MIT TLL · AAC&U
Before I let you go, one slide I would not have shown a year ago, but I have to show now. Everything I just showed you, any student in your course can do too. The Canvas REST API does not distinguish a faculty token from a student token. The same workflow I used to push pages can pull a student's own quiz questions and submit answers. Pretending that is not the case is the bigger risk than naming it. On the left, the reality. A student goes to Account, Settings, Approved Integrations, and mints a personal access token in under a minute. The same endpoints I demoed list quizzes, retrieve the questions and answer choices for that student's own in-progress attempt, and submit answers. Instructure publicly admitted this in October twenty twenty-five, in a product blog announcing tighter token controls. The quote, students setting up AI integrations to automatically review and complete assignments on their behalf. That is the platform vendor naming the problem. On the right, the answer. Notice what is not on this slide. LockDown Browser. Camera proctoring. Honor-code pledges. None of those see an API client running on a phone in the student's pocket. The durable fix is two layers. At the institution, a Canvas admin can disable student personal access tokens with one permission flip. One checkbox. Most institutions have not flipped it. I have flagged this in writing to our Innovation in Learning Center. At the course, the answer is assessment design. In-person proctored fact recall. Frequent low-stakes formative checkpoints. Project-based application. Oral defense. Detection is an arms race. Assessment design is not. The workflow I showed you is symmetrical. The answer to that symmetry is how you ask students to demonstrate learning, not what software you license to watch them.
